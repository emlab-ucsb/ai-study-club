Ocean physics · El Niño 2026

A foot of ocean is crossing the Pacific

A Kelvin wave is a slow, basin-wide bulge of warm water — hundreds of miles wide, about a foot tall, moving east at roughly walking pace. It is the machinery that builds an El Niño. One launched in the western Pacific this spring, and it is now rounding Mexico on its way to California.

What it is

Not a wave you can surf, or run from

A Kelvin wave is a large-scale wave trapped against a boundary — the equator, or a coastline — by the Earth's rotation. Because the Coriolis force vanishes at the equator and reverses sign across it, the equator acts like an invisible wall on both sides: a disturbance launched there cannot spread away, so it runs east along the waveguide instead, keeping its shape for thousands of miles. The same trapping happens against a coast, which is why the Pacific's waves take a right turn at South America and hug the Americas northward.

There are two families. The oceanic Kelvin wave is a pulse of warm water and a depressed thermocline, crossing the Pacific in about two months. The atmospheric (convectively coupled) Kelvin wave is a band of thunderstorms circling the tropics roughly five times faster, seeding rainfall bursts and tropical cyclones. Both are the same physics; only the fluid differs.

The news, September 2026

The wave is two weeks from California

A very strong El Niño is now established — the WMO put the odds of it lasting through February 2027 at close to 100% on 3 September, and NOAA's Climate Prediction Center gives better than 90% odds of a very strong event through the winter. The weekly Niño 3.4 index reached +2.7 °C in mid-August. That warmth was delivered by a sequence of downwelling Kelvin waves, and the latest one is now a coastal-trapped wave running north along Mexico at about 6 mph. Forecasters expect it off Southern California in early October.

Why a foot matters

Sea level is a stack, and the wave goes on top

A foot of extra water sounds survivable until you remember that coastal flooding is not driven by average sea level — it is driven by the sum of everything happening at once. The Kelvin wave does not replace the king tide or the winter storm; it raises the floor underneath them. A common coastal-engineering rule of thumb is that every four inches of added water roughly doubles how often a low-lying area floods.

Where the effects land

The wave is local; the El Niño it builds is not

The Kelvin wave's direct effect — elevated sea level — is confined to the eastern Pacific rim. But by shutting off cold upwelling it warms the eastern Pacific, and that warmth relocates the tropics' main rainfall engine thousands of miles east. Everything downstream of that shift moves with it: the Asian monsoon, the Atlantic hurricane season, the Panama Canal's water supply.

The other Kelvin wave

Oceanic Kelvin wave

In the water. Months.

A depression of the thermocline carrying warm water east along the equator at 2–3 m/s. Launched by westerly wind bursts, tracked by satellite altimeters and the TAO/TRITON buoy array. Consequence: sea level, sea surface temperature, and — over a season — El Niño itself.

Atmospheric Kelvin wave

In the air. Days.

A convectively coupled band of thunderstorms moving east at 11–14 m/s (occasionally up to 30). One of the largest sources of day-to-day rainfall variability in the tropics, a building block of the Madden–Julian Oscillation, and a known trigger for tropical cyclone formation. Consequence: which week it rains.

They are coupled. The wind bursts that launch oceanic Kelvin waves often ride along with the MJO and its atmospheric Kelvin waves — which is why a week of thunderstorms near Indonesia can end, two months later, as a foot of extra water in Malibu.
