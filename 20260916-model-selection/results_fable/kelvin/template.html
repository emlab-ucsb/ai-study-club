What a Kelvin wave is

A Kelvin wave is a huge, slow wave that rides along a boundary. In the ocean the "boundary" is either the equator (where the Coriolis effect flips sign) or a coastline. It shows up as a thick layer of warm water that pushes the thermocline down, lifting sea level by inches to a foot over thousands of miles.

1Trade winds slackenNormally easterly winds pile warm water up in the western Pacific. During a developing El Niño they weaken or reverse in bursts, and the piled-up water is released.
2The bulge crosses the PacificThe released warm water travels east along the equator as a downwelling Kelvin wave, about 2–3 m/s, taking two to three months to reach South America. It deepens the thermocline and warms the surface as it goes, feeding the El Niño.
3It splits at the coastHitting the Americas, the energy turns poleward as coastally trapped Kelvin waves, one branch north past Mexico and California, one south along Peru and Chile. That northward branch is what is in the news now.
