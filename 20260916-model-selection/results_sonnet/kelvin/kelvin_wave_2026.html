Kelvin Waves: The Hidden Pulse Behind the 2026 Super El Niño

A slow, basin-wide pulse of warm water is crossing the equatorial Pacific and peeling off a second wave that is now sliding up the West Coast — raising coastal sea levels for weeks at a time.

Reported conditions as of Sept 16, 2026 · forecasts evolve week to week

What is a Kelvin wave?

Not a surface swell — a slow-moving pulse thousands of miles wide that travels through the ocean (or atmosphere) trapped along the equator or a coastline. There are two kinds at work right now:

Equatorial Kelvin wave — forms when trade winds weaken or reverse, letting a pool of warm water that's normally piled up in the western Pacific slosh eastward, submerged 50–250 m below the surface. It takes about 2–3 months to cross the Pacific and is the main engine that builds an El Niño.
Coastal (poleward-trapped) Kelvin wave — when the equatorial wave hits South America, part of it turns and hugs the coastline, traveling north as a band of slightly elevated sea level at roughly 6 mph. It's this branch now approaching California.
