🌊 Kelvin Waves: An Ocean Phenomenon

Tracking a massive planetary-scale wave moving toward North America

What is a Kelvin Wave?

A Kelvin wave is an extremely large ocean wave that spans thousands of miles across the tropical Pacific. It's not a breaking wave or tsunami, but rather a movement of warm water and heat that travels beneath the ocean surface. When east-moving, it can raise sea levels along coasts and influence weather patterns globally.

Key Fact: A Kelvin wave takes 2-3 months to cross the tropical Pacific. The current wave originated near the equator and is moving northward along the North American coast, expected to reach California by late October 2026.

⚠️ Key Impacts & Concerns

🌊 Coastal Flooding

Higher tides combined with Kelvin wave elevation increase flood risk in low-lying coastal areas

🏖️ Beach Erosion

Elevated sea levels accelerate erosion of beaches and coastal bluffs, especially in California

🐟 Marine Ecosystems

Warmer waters disrupt fish distribution and suppress nutrient-rich upwelling that feeds food chains

🌡️ Ocean Temperature

Wave carries warm water, raising coastal temperatures and intensifying El Niño effects

🔗 Connection to El Niño

Kelvin waves are intimately connected to El Niño, a climate pattern that affects global weather. When a warm (downwelling) Kelvin wave travels eastward across the Pacific, it can:

  • Transport subsurface heat toward the coast, contributing to El Niño development or strengthening
  • Suppress the cold-water upwelling that normally brings nutrient-rich deep water to the surface
  • Influence rainfall patterns and atmospheric circulation across the Pacific basin
  • Affect global temperature and precipitation patterns for months
Current Situation: Scientists are closely monitoring this Kelvin wave because it could contribute to continued El Niño warming and intensify coastal hazards already exacerbated by climate change and previous storms.

🛡️ What You Should Know

  • Not a tsunami: Kelvin waves travel underwater slowly with minimal surface disturbance
  • Predictable: Scientists can forecast its arrival with reasonable accuracy
  • Compounding effects: Most dangerous when combined with storm surge, high tide, or heavy rain
  • Regional impact: Affects coastal communities most; inland areas unaffected
  • Multi-month duration: Effects persist as wave passes and gradually dissipates
